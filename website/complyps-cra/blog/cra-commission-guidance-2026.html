The Commission's CRA guidance: what SMEs need to know

The regulation tells you what. The Commission's 2026 guidance helps with how — especially for smaller manufacturers.

On 27 July 2026 the European Commission published guidance to support timely implementation of the Cyber Resilience Act (Communication C(2026) 6013). It is non-binding — the regulation itself remains the law — but it is the clearest official signal yet of how the Commission reads key provisions, and it includes 67 practical examples aimed at micro, small and medium-sized enterprises.

Why it matters: many CRA questions are not "what does the law say?" but "does it apply to my situation?" — a cloud backend, an open-source library, a firmware update. The guidance is organised around exactly those questions.

The topics it covers

1. Scope — including remote data processing and open source

The CRA applies to products with digital elements made available on the EU market. Two grey areas get particular attention:

  • Remote data processing. Under the CRA, a remote data processing solution can be part of a product when the product cannot perform one of its functions without it — think of the cloud backend a smart device depends on. If that applies to you, the backend falls within your CRA responsibilities.
  • Free and open-source software. Open-source software is in scope only when it is made available on the market in the course of a commercial activity. Developers who contribute code to open-source projects that are not under their responsibility are outside the CRA. Organisations that provide sustained support for open source intended for commercial use may be open-source stewards — with lighter obligations (a cybersecurity policy, cooperation with authorities, and reporting from 11 December 2027) and no administrative fines.

2. Substantial modification

If you change a product after placing it on the market in a way that affects its compliance with the essential requirements, or modifies its intended purpose, it can be treated as a new product — and whoever makes the change may take on manufacturer obligations. This is a practical question for anyone shipping feature updates or refurbishing devices.

3. Support periods

Manufacturers must set a support period during which vulnerabilities are handled and security updates provided. Under the CRA it should reflect how long the product is expected to be used, and be at least five years unless the product is expected to be in use for less time. The support period end date must be made clear to users.

4. Reporting

The guidance addresses the reporting obligations that have applied since 11 September 2026 — what counts as an actively exploited vulnerability or a severe incident, and how the 24-hour, 72-hour and final reports work through the ENISA Single Reporting Platform. (See our article on CRA reporting being live.)

5. Risk assessment

Everything in the CRA flows from a cybersecurity risk assessment of the product: it decides which essential requirements apply and how. The assessment must be documented, kept up to date throughout the support period, and included in the technical documentation.

What to do with it

  • Find your examples. Skim the guidance for cases that look like your products — it is the fastest way to resolve scope questions.
  • Record your reasoning. Where the guidance supports a scope or support-period decision, note it in your technical documentation.
  • Watch for updates. The Commission's guidance page has continued to be updated (most recently in November 2026), and harmonised standards for the CRA are still being developed.
